01The Brief

A recipe page nobody can touch.

The brief: redesign a tablet recipe page into a "Cook-Along Mode" for users whose hands are covered in flour, oil, or water — mid-recipe, tablet propped against the backsplash, three to five feet away.

Original brief

"Redesign a tablet recipe page into a Cook-Along Mode for users whose hands are covered in flour, oil, or water. Legibility optimised for a 3–5 ft viewing distance. Propose non-standard, zero-touch inputs. Manage multiple active timers and ingredient scaling without clutter."

👁️
Legibility

Optimised for a viewing distance of 3–5 feet — no squinting, no leaning in with a dirty hand.

🚫
Zero-Touch Interaction

Standard taps are off the table. Needs voice, camera gesture, or high-affordance "elbow" targets.

🧠
Cognitive Load

Multiple active timers and scaled ingredients, managed without ever cluttering the screen.

02Principles

Four decisions that carry the design.

Apple's design language isn't about adding affordances — it's about removing everything that isn't the current decision. Applied to a kitchen counter, that means one instruction, one glance, and inputs that assume the user's hands are unusable.

01
One step fills the screen — never the full recipe

The ingredient list and thirty-step method disappear the moment Cook-Along starts. Only the current step, in type large enough to read from 3–5 ft, stays on screen. Progress is a thin bar, not a scroll position to track.

02
Three tiers of hands-free input, escalating with the kitchen's noise

Voice is primary and fastest. If the extractor fan or a blender drowns it out, an air gesture at the front camera works instead. If neither lands, a full-height "elbow zone" on each screen edge is always visible and always tappable with a knuckle, forearm, or elbow.

03
Timers live in a dock, not on the stage

Active timers collapse into small chips that sit above the step, glanceable but never competing with it. A timer only claims the full screen at the one moment it actually needs a decision: when it finishes.

04
The system shows what it heard before it acts

Because the user can't glance down to correct a misfire with a tap, every voice or gesture command surfaces a caption first — confirming the command that's about to run, not just executing silently.

🎤Voice

Primary input. Always listening — the orb pulses to confirm, and a caption shows what it heard before acting.

👋Air Gesture

Fallback for a loud kitchen. The front camera reads a swipe in the air — no need to get close or speak.

💪Elbow Target

Fallback of last resort. Two full-height glass zones on the frame itself — click them directly, the way a forearm would press them.

04Storyboard

How the logic plays out at the counter.

Six moments across one recipe — from clean hands starting the mode, to a fallback gesture in a noisy kitchen, to a timer interrupting only when it has to.

01🧼
Hands clean, hands free

Before touching a single ingredient, the cook taps "Start Cook-Along" — the last touch input the session will ever need.

02🎯
One step, full focus

The full recipe disappears. Step one fills the screen in type readable from across the counter; the listening orb switches on.

03🗣️
Hands get messy

Dough on both hands, the cook says "next step" from four feet away. A caption confirms the words before the step actually advances.

04👋
No mic? No problem

The extractor fan is roaring. The cook waves at the camera instead — a ghost trail confirms the swipe was seen and understood.

05⏱️
Two timers, zero clutter

Potatoes and salmon roast on different clocks. Both sit quietly as small chips in the dock while the cook keeps working the current step.

06🔔
Only interrupt when it matters

The moment a timer hits zero, it's the one thing allowed to take the full screen — cleared with a word, a wave, or an elbow.

05Outcome
Design POV

Removing the touchscreen was the feature.

The hardest part of this brief wasn't inventing new gestures — it was resisting the urge to keep the full recipe visible "just in case." Every constraint here (distance, dirty hands, competing timers) pointed to the same answer: show one decision at a time, and make the fallback inputs as dignified as the primary one, not an apologetic afterthought.

That's the same instinct behind Apple's best single-purpose interfaces — a boarding pass, a workout ring, a timer on a watch face. Reduce until only the next decision is left on screen.
